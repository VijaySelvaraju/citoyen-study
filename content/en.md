## Page 6 — The motto and symbols of the Republic

France's motto, **“Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité” — “Liberty, Equality, Fraternity”**, expresses the founding values of the French Republic and provides shared reference points for living together. It appears on public buildings such as prefectures and town halls.

### A. Liberty

Women and men are free in their choices, opinions and ways of thinking, expressing themselves and living.

Liberty is a fundamental value enshrined in the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789 (DDHC)**. Article 4 states that liberty consists in being able to do anything that does not harm others. In other words, people may do what does not interfere with others, within limits defined by law.

All citizens have the same freedoms, subject to the law. These freedoms are discussed in the section on citizens' rights and duties.

Illustration: the Declaration of 1789.

### B. Equality

Equality is a fundamental principle of the French Republic (Article 1 of the Constitution of 1958). All citizens have the same rights regardless of sex, origin, religion, opinions or sexual orientation (Article 1 of the DDHC).

Equality between women and men has been enshrined in the constitutional preamble since 1946. It applies in every field, including employment, health and education.

France condemns discrimination. Discrimination means treating one person differently from another in a comparable situation—housing, work or leisure, for example—because of their origin, sex, sexual orientation, political opinions, age, religious beliefs or physical appearance. Discrimination is punishable by law.

## Page 7 — Equality and fraternity

The State actively combats:

- **Sexism:** demeaning treatment of a person because of their sex.
- **Racism:** discrimination based on a person's origin or ethnic or racial affiliation.
- **Antisemitism:** a specific form of racism and hatred directed at Jews, based on prejudice, stereotypes and discrimination.
- **Religious discrimination:** discrimination based on a person's presumed religion.
- **Anti-LGBT+ hatred and discrimination:** hostility towards people because of their sexual orientation.

**Sidebar:** An employer's decisions, such as recruitment or promotion, must be based on professional rather than personal considerations.

### C. Fraternity

France is founded on its citizens' desire to live together. This is expressed through fraternity between everyone. Fraternity means being able to recognise another person as a fellow human being despite differences. Republican fraternity is civic; it is not based on origin or religion.

Fraternity entails solidarity between individuals in different forms:

- **Collective solidarity:** the nation protects individuals, for example through Social Security. Meeting tax obligations is an act of solidarity that finances public services for everyone's benefit. Solidarity also requires French citizens to take part in national defence when necessary.
- **Solidarity between generations:** generations support one another. For example, employees' contributions help pay pensions.

Fraternity therefore helps reduce inequalities, particularly social inequalities.

**Sidebar:** Organising collections for humanitarian organisations or volunteering in a neighbourhood association are further examples of civic participation that put solidarity and fraternity into practice.

## Page 8 — Symbols of France

### D. France's symbols

- **National day:** On 14 July 1789, the people of Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal prison, marking the beginning of the French Revolution. A year later, on 14 July 1790, the Fête de la Fédération was celebrated. Since 1880, 14 July has been the national holiday commemorating these two events.
- **National anthem:** Rouget de Lisle wrote *La Marseillaise* in 1792. Children study it in primary school. Its words follow on page 9.
- **Official language:** French is the official language of the Republic.
- **Marianne:** A woman wearing a Phrygian cap, a symbol of liberty, represents the Republic. She appears on French administrative documents. Her bust in town halls recalls the French Revolution.
- **Tricolour flag:** The blue, white and red flag was also created during the Revolution. It became France's official flag in 1794, except during the Restoration (1814–1830), discussed in the history section.
- **Gallic rooster:** The rooster represents pride, courage and vigilance. Although it has no official constitutional status, it has been used since antiquity and is widely recognised as a national symbol.

These symbols appear on government websites and documents. Illustrations show Marianne and the façade of the Finistère prefecture.

## Page 10 — Principles of the Republic

Article 1 of the Constitution of 4 October 1958 defines France as **an indivisible, secular, democratic and social Republic**.

A constitution is a state's founding legal instrument: fundamental rules defining how the country is organised and governed. In a state governed by the rule of law, the constitution stands at the top of the hierarchy of legal rules, above ordinary laws. In France it establishes citizens' rights and freedoms, explains how powers are divided between government, Parliament and the courts, and governs how they operate.

### A. An indivisible Republic

No individual or group may decide on behalf of all French people. Decisions are made by representatives elected by the people, or by the people themselves in referendums. Throughout metropolitan and overseas France, French citizens are subject to the same laws and have the same rights and duties: this is the unity of the Republic. French is the country's sole official language.

### B. A secular Republic — laïcité

Laïcité is an essential constitutional principle, officially celebrated on 9 December. It guarantees everyone's freedom of conscience: each person may have no religion, have a religion, convert to another religion or cease to have a religion.

It protects the freedom to express beliefs or convictions and practise a religion, subject to respect for public order, as stated in Article 10 of the DDHC of 1789. It guarantees freedom of worship and respect for all beliefs, and requires the State to remain neutral.

**Sidebar:** The source describes an atheist as someone who does not believe in any religion, and an agnostic as someone who is sceptical about religion.

The following pages explain how laïcité applies in different settings.

## Page 11 — State neutrality, proselytism and blasphemy

### 1. State neutrality

Laïcité means that the State and religious organisations are separate, under the 1905 law separating Churches and State. The law applies equally to everyone, regardless of religion or beliefs.

The State does not support any religious organisation, treats them equally and does not intervene in their internal affairs. For example, it cannot finance the construction of religious buildings or pay religious personnel. There are exceptions, including historical arrangements with certain religions in Alsace and Moselle.

**Sidebar:** The State may finance restoration of a religious monument classified as heritage.

The State, local authorities and public services are religiously neutral. The Republic guarantees equal treatment by public administration and public services, regardless of religion or convictions. This is State neutrality.

### 2. Proselytism

Religious proselytism means trying to persuade someone to adopt a religion or religious ideas. It is permitted in France, provided nobody uses violence, threats or pressure to force another person to believe against their will. The booklet also states that proselytism in public services is prohibited under the principle of State neutrality.

**Sidebar:** Religious leaflets may be distributed in the street as long as public order is not disturbed.

### 3. Blasphemy

Blasphemy is speech that insults a deity, religion or something regarded as sacred or worthy of respect. It is not an offence in France. Everyone may criticise or mock a religion and express themselves freely, including through cartoons. Nobody may be punished for criticising a religion or insulting a deity: this is freedom of expression.

## Page 12 — Limits to expression; laïcité in public spaces and services

Freedom of expression has limits, but blasphemy is not one of them. Those limits concern:

- Defamation—false statements intended to harm someone—and public insults.
- Encouraging certain crimes or offences against a religion.
- Inciting racial, ethnic or religious hatred.
- Using religion to justify war crimes or terrorism.
- Inciting discrimination.
- Denying crimes against humanity, including Holocaust denial.

### 4. Applying laïcité in different settings

#### a. Public spaces

Everyone may display their religion in streets, parks, public gardens, markets and public transport. People may wear religious symbols such as a veil, kippah, cross or turban; participate in religious celebrations or gatherings; and express their beliefs.

Public order must nevertheless be respected. A religious gathering may require authorisation, be regulated or be prohibited to prevent public disorder; the source gives street prayers as an example.

**Sidebar:** The law of 11 October 2010 prohibits concealing the face in public spaces, including with balaclavas, masks or full-face veils, to protect public order. [E11]

#### b. Public services

People working in public services, such as hospitals, schools and town halls, must remain neutral and not display personal opinions or beliefs while working. This helps ensure fair treatment of service users.

Neutrality also applies to public-service buildings: religious, political or philosophical symbols must not be displayed in offices, at counters or in common areas.

This neutrality requirement does not apply in the same way to service users. Users may wear religious symbols if they respect the service's operating rules. They cannot demand changes to a public service in the name of religion.

## Page 13 — Laïcité at work and in public schools

#### c. The workplace

Different rules apply to public-service staff and private-sector employees. To guarantee State neutrality, public employees cannot display religious beliefs or opinions at work. They cannot wear religious symbols or practise their religion while working.

Private-sector employees may express religious opinions, including through clothing or symbols, unless this disrupts the company's operation or raises safety issues. An employer may impose restrictions in workplace rules when justified by the nature of the employee's duties.

**Sidebar:** Employees in either sector may request leave for a religious holiday. An employer may refuse for non-discriminatory reasons, including workplace rules or service requirements.

#### d. Public schools

Public schools and national education staff are religiously neutral. Laïcité at school protects pupils' freedom of conscience, allowing them to think independently and develop their opinions without pressure or influence.

The **Charte de la laïcité à l'École** sets out the rules. It addresses school staff, including teachers and extracurricular activity leaders, pupils and parents.

Under the law of 15 March 2004, conspicuous religious signs or clothing—those that immediately display religious affiliation—are prohibited. Discreet signs are permitted. Religion cannot be used to refuse a school's operating rules or a subject on the curriculum.

## Page 14 — A democratic and social Republic

### C. A democratic Republic

France is a democracy: national sovereignty, or power, belongs to the French people. Article 3 of the 1958 Constitution states that the people exercise sovereignty through representatives and by referendum.

A republic is a political system in which leaders are elected and govern in the people's name. The President, parliamentarians, mayors and representatives of regions, departments and municipalities are elected by direct or indirect universal suffrage. Citizens aged at least 18, with civil and political rights, may vote and stand for election under the conditions established by law.

**Sidebar:** Direct universal suffrage means citizens elect their representatives directly. Under indirect universal suffrage, representatives elected by citizens in turn elect other representatives.

### D. A social Republic

The Republic's social character follows from equality. Article 1 of the DDHC of 1789 declares that human beings are born and remain free and equal in rights. To make that equality real, the State promotes equal opportunity and improves the circumstances of the poorest and most vulnerable, particularly through:

- Education: free schooling.
- Housing: social-housing policy.
- Employment: income support (*revenu de solidarité active*, RSA) and help returning to work.
- Health: Social Security.

## Page 16 — Democracy and the rule of law

France is a democracy governed by the rule of law (*État de droit*): everyone, including the State, must respect the law. The rule of law is a condition of democracy and contrasts with arbitrary government.

Article 2 of the 1958 Constitution expresses democracy as “government of the people, by the people and for the people”. Power is exercised through institutions organised by the Constitution, approved in France by referendum—a direct vote of the citizens.

The current Constitution is that of the Fifth Republic, adopted in 1958. State decisions must respect it and the DDHC of 1789, referred to in its preamble. The Constitution also defines the State's organisation.

**Sidebar:** Constitutional amendments must be approved by referendum or by Parliament meeting jointly in Congress.

The rule of law rests on protection of fundamental freedoms, separation of the three powers and judicial independence:

- **Legislative power:** drafting and passing laws, exercised by deputies and senators.
- **Executive power:** defining national policy and implementing laws, exercised by government.
- **Judicial power:** administering justice, exercised by judges.

Political parties may form and operate freely. Public administration must also respect the Constitution, laws and republican principles, under judicial supervision.

### A. Main State institutions

- **President of the Republic:** Head of State and of the armed forces, elected by direct universal suffrage for five years, “renewable once” in the source's wording. [E1] On the Prime Minister's proposal, the President appoints government ministers; the government is described as emerging from the legislative-election majority. The Prime Minister directs its work.
- **Parliament:** The National Assembly (*Assemblée nationale*) and Senate (*Sénat*) pass laws and oversee government. The 577 deputies are directly elected for five years in legislative elections. The 348 senators are indirectly elected for six years.

**Sidebar:** A presidential candidate must obtain endorsements from 500 elected officials.

## Page 17 — Justice and voting

- **Justice:** Judges settle disputes between individuals or businesses, and between citizens and public administration. They punish breaches of the law with legally prescribed penalties, such as imprisonment or fines.
- **Constitutional Council (*Conseil constitutionnel*):** It checks that laws comply with the Constitution and supervises the regularity of national elections.

### B. Voting rights

Voting allows citizens to express their will in an election. Suffrage is universal. The first election under universal suffrage took place in 1848, but only adult men could vote. French women gained voting rights in April 1944 and voted for the first time in April 1945.

To vote, a person must:

- Have French nationality, or nationality of an EU member state for municipal and European Parliament elections.
- Be at least 18.
- Not have been deprived of civic rights.
- Be registered on electoral rolls, online or at the town hall of their place of residence.

If a condition is not met, the person cannot vote.

**Sidebar:** Deprivation of civic rights is a penalty for serious offences that removes certain rights, such as voting or standing for election.

Voting is personal and secret. Voters enter a booth, put their ballot into an envelope, then place it in the ballot box. The source says multiple people may not enter a booth together or communicate between booths. Candidates have designated election-poster boards in each municipality; damaging them is prohibited.

**Question: What document must I show at the polling station?** French and EU citizens must present identification. See https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F1361. Identification is not compulsory in municipalities of fewer than 1,000 inhabitants, but the polling-station chair may ask for proof of identity by any means if in doubt.

## Page 19 — Territorial organisation of the Republic

Under decentralisation, the State transfers responsibilities and resources to **local authorities (*collectivités territoriales*)**: municipalities, departments, regions and overseas authorities. Regional and departmental prefects represent the State locally; high commissioners do so in certain overseas territories.

### A. Municipalities — communes

The source counts **34,875 municipalities on 1 January 2025**. Each is governed by a mayor and a municipal council elected for six years. French citizens and nationals of other EU countries may vote and stand in municipal elections.

Municipal responsibilities include public nursery and elementary schools, sport and culture, municipal roads, and civil-status registers recording births, marriages and deaths. Groups of municipalities (*intercommunalités*, including communities of municipalities and metropolitan authorities) manage services such as water, sanitation, waste and urban transport.

### B. Departments — départements

Departmental councils are responsible particularly for public lower-secondary schools (*collèges*), social services, departmental roads, child protection and assistance to older people. France has **101 departments: 96 metropolitan and five overseas**. Mayotte, created as a department in 2011, is the newest.

### C. Regions — régions

France has **18 regions: 13 metropolitan and five overseas**, administered by regional councils. Responsibilities include public transport, economic development, vocational training, and construction and maintenance of public upper-secondary schools (*lycées*).

## Page 22 — The European Union and its institutions

France helped initiate European integration. Following the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951, the Treaty of Rome established the European Economic Community in 1957 between six founding countries, including France. ## Page 23 — European citizenship, symbols and milestones

Since 1992, French citizens have also been European citizens, according to the booklet's chronology. [E2] Every five years they elect members of the European Parliament, which sits in Strasbourg, France. ## Page 25 — Fundamental rights

Being a state's citizen means having its nationality and, with it, rights and duties. These shape life in society and maintain a balance between individual freedoms and the common good. This chapter first explores fundamental rights, then the duties accompanying them.

Some rights and duties are specific to French citizens, including voting, national defence and service on a criminal jury (*jury d'assises*). Most rights and duties, however, apply to everyone living in France, regardless of nationality or status.

Laws and regulations apply to all. For example, the **Civil Code (*Code civil*)** defines essential rules governing relations between people. The **Criminal Code (*Code pénal*)** defines offences and their penalties.

**Sidebar:** Napoléon Bonaparte promulgated the Civil Code in **1804**.

Fundamental rights are principally guaranteed by:

- The Constitution of the Fifth Republic, particularly its preamble.
- The DDHC of 1789.
- The preamble to the Constitution of 1946.
- The Environmental Charter of 2004.
- International instruments ratified by France, such as the European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms, commonly called the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR/CEDH).

**Where can you find current laws and rules?** Laws, often organised into codes, are available at https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr. The site https://www.vie-publique.fr explains major rules and how institutions work.

## Page 26 — Rights relating to individual liberty

### A. Individual liberty

#### 1. Freedom of choice

Freedom of choice is a fundamental personal freedom: people may decide their way of life, relationships and plans within the law. It is protected by the 1958 Constitution, whose preamble refers to the DDHC of 1789. Article 4 defines liberty as doing anything that does not harm others.

#### 2. Freedom of thought, conscience and religion

The Constitution protects freedom of thought, notably through Article 10 of the DDHC of 1789: nobody should be troubled for their opinions, including religious opinions, provided their expression does not disturb public order established by law.

Freedom of thought and conscience allow everyone to believe, reflect and doubt without coercion. Freedom of thought is complete. People may choose and practise a religion, believe in one or several gods, or have no religion. The section on laïcité develops this subject. Anyone is also free to join a political party.

**Sidebar:** People in prison retain freedom of worship during detention.

#### 3. Freedom of expression

Everyone may express ideas and opinions within the limits set by law. Calls for violence are prohibited, as are insults or threats directed at someone for expressing themselves. The State may restrict expression, particularly to prevent discriminatory statements. Expression on social media is protected, but content inciting hatred or violence is prohibited.

Illustration: Eugène Delacroix's *Liberty Leading the People* (1830).

## Page 27 — Press freedom and freedom of movement

### Focus: freedom of the press

Under Article 11 of the DDHC, free communication of thoughts and opinions is a fundamental principle. The press is free in France. Journalists may inform the public about events in France and worldwide, express opinions and publish information concerning institutions, public figures and religious beliefs.

Press freedom is regulated by law. Journalists may not publish defamatory statements, incite crimes or offences, or deny crimes against humanity. Doing so may lead to prosecution.

**Question: Can you say anything publicly?** Expression is a fundamental right but has limits protecting others' rights. Insults, defamation, incitement to hatred and glorification of crimes against humanity are prohibited.

### 4. Freedom of movement

Citizens may travel within France and the EU, and freely enter and leave the country. Minors travelling without their parents need authorisation to leave the territory. In principle, French citizens' movements within France are not subject to checks.

The **Schengen area** allows movement without checks at internal borders. The booklet describes it as comprising 23 EU countries and four associated countries—Norway, Iceland, Switzerland and Liechtenstein. [E4]

Exceptions to freedom of movement include:

- Prisoners, who lose freedom of movement for the duration of their sentence.
- Non-European foreigners, whose entry is subject to states' sovereign conditions, including visas.
- Exceptional and temporary crises, such as a state of emergency, during which the State may restrict movement.

**Sidebar:** The source states that non-EU foreigners must hold a residence permit after an unrenewed visa expires. This is a general description; applicable residence arrangements depend on status.

## Page 28 — Rights relating to dignity

### B. Dignity

#### 1. Respect for private life

The Constitution guarantees privacy. It includes personal intimacy, medical confidentiality and image rights; limits on surveillance and investigations such as telephone interception; and rules reducing risks associated with digital tools.

Courts safeguard privacy. Protections include:

- **Professional and medical confidentiality:** a doctor may not reveal a person's medical information without consent.
- **Intimacy:** information about romantic relationships or sexual preferences may not be disclosed without the person's knowledge.
- **Image rights:** privacy and image rights are distinct but closely related. The source prohibits photographing, filming or recording someone without authorisation in a private place, or a public place where they may reasonably expect not to be observed or heard.

**Question: What can I do if my privacy is violated?** File a complaint with the national police or gendarmerie.

#### 2. Human dignity

A person must never be treated as an object or merely as a means. The principle is enshrined in the preamble to the Constitution of 1946.

The EU Charter of Fundamental Rights identifies five essential protections:

- Human dignity.
- The right to life.
- Physical and mental integrity.
- Prohibition of torture and inhuman or degrading treatment.
- Prohibition of slavery and forced labour.

**Example:** A person employed by a company but not paid is in a situation that violates dignity.

## Page 29 — Bodily autonomy and security

### 3. The right to control one's own body

Everyone may make decisions about their body, especially medical decisions, while respecting integrity and dignity. Rape, mutilation, forced sterilisation and female genital mutilation are prohibited and punishable violations of this right.

Several laws and public-health measures protect bodily autonomy. Access to contraception, including for minors, allows people to avoid pregnancy and choose not to become parents.

Since **8 March 2024**, the Constitution guarantees freedom to obtain an abortion (*interruption volontaire de grossesse*, IVG). A pregnant woman, including a minor, may request an abortion under the conditions set by law.

Having a child is also a personal choice. The bioethics law of **2 August 2021** permits medically assisted reproduction (*procréation médicalement assistée*, PMA) for all women, including heterosexual couples, lesbian couples and single women. Surrogacy (*gestation pour autrui*, GPA), where another woman carries a child for a couple, is not authorised and is described in the source as an offence.

**Sidebar:** Vaccination is compulsory for children. In other respects, people are free to refuse medical treatment or procedures; this is the booklet's general statement.

### C. Security and justice

#### 1. Security of the person — sûreté

This protects people from arbitrary arrest, detention or punishment. Nobody may be arrested or imprisoned without a legal basis and compliance with legal procedures. A judge supervises deprivation of liberty.

The police and gendarmerie maintain public order and safety, investigate crimes, and provide prevention and assistance. Under judicial supervision, they may arrest, detain, question and search people. Anyone arrested or placed in police custody must be informed of the reasons and their rights, including remaining silent, receiving a lawyer's assistance and notifying someone close to them.

## Page 30 — Justice and political rights

### 2. Access to justice

Everyone may assert their rights in court and is entitled to a fair trial before independent, impartial judges. Everyone has the right to be heard, defend themselves, receive a lawyer's assistance and be treated equally. The presumption of innocence applies until a final conviction. Penalties must be prescribed by law and proportionate to the alleged acts.

These principles ensure fair, transparent justice that respects fundamental rights. Support includes:

- **Legal aid (*aide juridictionnelle*):** State financial assistance for people with limited means who need a lawyer in proceedings before a French court.
- **Defender of Rights (*Défenseur des droits*):** An independent administrative authority created in 2011, dealing with disputes involving administration, discrimination and infringements of rights. It protects public-service users' rights, among others. Local delegates receive people.
- **Legal advice centres (*maisons de justice et du droit*):** They provide guidance, with free sessions run by specialist associations under departmental access-to-law councils (CDAD).

### D. Political rights

#### 1. Voting and standing for election

These are foundations of representative democracy. The institutional-system and republican-principles sections explain how they are exercised.

#### 2. Freedom of assembly

Everyone may gather peacefully to express opinions, debate or defend common interests. This is essential to democracy. If there is a risk of public disorder, such as violence or riots, administrative authorities, including mayors or prefects, may restrict it under judicial supervision.

## Page 31 — Demonstrations, associations and the environment

### 3. Freedom to demonstrate

Demonstrations are a traditional form of collective expression in French political and social life. This fundamental freedom is recognised through the DDHC and French law. Organisers must notify the administration beforehand to prevent public disorder, identifying the organisers and proposed route.

### 4. Freedom of association

The law of **1 July 1901** recognises the right to create or join an association, protecting individuals' freedoms and rights while enabling collective action. Associations must respect republican laws; an association that does not respect republican principles may be dissolved by the State.

### E. Environmental rights

Everyone has the right to live in a balanced environment that respects health. This is recognised by the **Environmental Charter of 2004**, incorporated into the Constitution of 1958.

## Page 32 — Obligations and duties: respecting the law

Fundamental rights come with duties ensuring social cohesion, respect for law and a functioning democracy. These duties arise from the Constitution, laws and republican principles.

Everyone living in France must obey current laws, which protect public order and safety. Courts ensure proper application and may impose legal penalties for breaches. Offences fall into three categories according to seriousness:

### 2. Respect institutions

Respect for French institutions recognises the authority of the State, local authorities and courts, which underpin republican organisation. It requires respecting public employees, local elected officials and judges in their functions.

## Page 34 — Values, democratic participation and taxation

### B. Respect republican principles and values

Being French involves sharing liberty, equality and fraternity and committing to respect them in everyday and social life. Part 1 explains these principles.

### C. Participate in democratic life

#### 1. Voting

Voting is not compulsory in France, unlike in some countries, but it is an important civic duty that gives elected representatives legitimacy. Part 2 explains the democratic system.

#### 2. Respect election results

Respecting election results is essential to democracy and the rule of law. Results bind every citizen, whatever their political opinions.

### D. Contribute to public expenditure

Financing public services is a constitutional duty: residents contribute according to their means. Article 13 of the DDHC of 1789 states that a common contribution is essential for the public force and administrative expenditure.

All residents, not only French nationals, contribute to services such as transport, shared facilities, education, health and security. The booklet therefore states that every adult resident no longer included in their parents' tax household must declare all income annually and pay taxes. False declarations to French administration may be penalised. Tax liability itself depends on the applicable rules and circumstances.

## Page 35 — National defence and the environment

### E. Defend the country and national security

National defence relies principally on professional armed forces and nuclear deterrence. Compulsory military service has been suspended since 1997. Young citizens participate in a **Defence and Citizenship Day (*Journée défense et citoyenneté*, JDC)**, learning about national-security responsibilities and the armed forces' work in peace and war.

Voluntary reserves also allow people to serve the public interest: military, police, civic and health reserves. In a crisis or war, every citizen may be called upon to contribute to national defence.

## Page 36 — Education, mutual help and solidarity

### G. Education and transmitting values

#### 1. Compulsory education

Education is compulsory from **3 to 16**. Parents must enrol children from the school year beginning in the calendar year of their third birthday. Failure to comply may lead to prosecution. Part 5 provides more detail.

#### 2. Transmitting republican values

Liberty, equality, fraternity and laïcité are important in children's education. Schools and families share this responsibility. Parents are the first educators and play a major role in transmitting values in daily life.

Helping others reflects fraternity. It can be a legal obligation, including assisting a person in danger when doing so does not endanger one's own life. Failure to assist is an offence.

Beyond legal duties, individual initiatives and participation in associations strengthen social ties and help people live together.

## Page 39 — Middle Ages and Ancien Régime

### Middle Ages: fifth to fifteenth centuries

**Clovis (466–511)** united several kingdoms inhabited by Frankish peoples, an important step towards territorial unity. He adopted Christianity.

**Joan of Arc (*Jeanne d'Arc*, 1412–1431)** was a young peasant who played a major role in the Hundred Years' War between France and England. She led French troops in liberating part of the territory and became a national heroine symbolising patriotism and courage.

### Ancien Régime: 1492–1789

Absolute monarchy characterised the Ancien Régime: the king held all powers. Kings were crowned in Reims Cathedral. A strong, centralised state developed.

**Henri IV (1553–1610)** issued the **Edict of Nantes in 1598**, ending the religious wars between Catholics and Protestants.

**Louis XIV**, the “Sun King” (*Roi-Soleil*), reigned from **1643 to 1715**. He had the Palace of Versailles built near Paris, a jewel of classical architecture.

**Molière** was a seventeenth-century playwright and actor during Louis XIV's reign, famous for comedies such as *L'Avare* (*The Miser*) and *Le Bourgeois gentilhomme*.

**Enlightenment philosophers**—Rousseau, Voltaire, Diderot, Montesquieu, Condorcet and d'Alembert—used their writings to defend tolerance and freedom of thought and oppose arbitrary power. Their ideas influenced the Revolution's ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity. Montesquieu, in particular, denounced slavery.

## Page 40 — Revolution and First Republic

### French Revolution: 1789–1792

On **14 July 1789**, Parisians seized the Bastille, a royal prison symbolising arbitrary monarchical power. This marked the beginning of the Revolution and led to the end of absolute monarchy.

The Revolution gave rise to republican values through the DDHC of 1789, whose drafting involved the **Marquis de La Fayette**.

A constitutional monarchy was established in **1791**, limiting **Louis XVI**'s powers. His attempted flight abroad and social tensions turned the population against him. He was overthrown on **10 August 1792**, tried and guillotined in **1793** on what is now Place de la Concorde.

### First Republic: 1792–1804

The First Republic began in **1792**, succeeding constitutional monarchy and inheriting the Revolution. France faced domestic revolts and wars abroad. The government introduced the **Terror**, closely monitoring perceived enemies of the Revolution and executing suspected traitors by guillotine.

**Maximilien Robespierre** defended equality and the Republic, but also led the Terror. **Napoléon Bonaparte** took power in **1799**, ended the First Republic in **1804** and proclaimed the First Empire.

## Page 41 — First Empire, Restoration and July Monarchy

### First Empire: 1804–1815

Napoléon became emperor in **1804**. He governed France for about 15 years overall and introduced reforms that still shape the State, including the **Civil Code**, **Banque de France** and the office of **prefect**.

He fought many European wars to expand his empire and centralised power by controlling the press and opponents. He restored slavery in **1802**, then abolished the slave trade in **1815**. Weakened by military defeats, he abdicated definitively in **1815** after Waterloo. The Empire ended and monarchy returned.

### Restoration and July Monarchy: 1815–1848 in this section

The Restoration began in 1815 with **Louis XVIII**'s return, according to this page. He restored monarchy while retaining a constitutional charter protecting freedoms such as the press and parliamentary elections. His conservative measures strengthened the Church and monitored opponents. The timeline on page 38 also includes the initial restoration of 1814.

**Charles X** succeeded him in **1824**. In **1830**, Parisians revolted against excessive restrictions on freedom. The Restoration ended and a more liberal monarchy—the **July Monarchy**—began under **Louis-Philippe**.

Louis-Philippe strengthened Parliament and guaranteed more freedoms, including press freedom. Power nevertheless relied mainly on the bourgeoisie, and voting remained restricted to the wealthy. Growing social inequality and refusal to widen voting rights created tensions. Another revolution in **1848** ended the regime.

## Page 42 — Second Republic, slavery and Second Empire

### Second Republic: 1848–1852

The Second Republic followed the Restoration and July Monarchy. A major event was the **abolition of slavery in 1848**. **Victor Schœlcher**, Under-Secretary of State responsible for the colonies, drafted the abolition decree.

**Victor Hugo (1802–1885)**, one of France's greatest writers, opposed social inequality and the death penalty. His famous works include *Les Misérables* and *Notre-Dame de Paris*.

### Slavery

From the seventeenth century, slavery developed through the transatlantic trade and colonial exploitation overseas. The booklet describes the forced deportation of **1.2 million Africans** to colonies in the Antilles, including Saint-Domingue, Martinique and Guadeloupe, as well as Réunion and French Guiana. Enslaved people worked mainly on sugar, coffee and other plantations in extremely harsh conditions. Nantes was France's principal transatlantic slave-trading port. Slavery continued in these colonies until 1848.

The **Taubira law of 2001** recognises the slave trade and slavery as crimes against humanity. **10 May** is the national day commemorating the slave trade, slavery and their abolitions.

### Second Empire: 1852–1870

**Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte**, Napoléon I's nephew and elected president, staged a coup in **December 1851** and became **Emperor Napoléon III in 1852**.

Initially authoritarian, the regime became more liberal in the 1860s, granting people more freedoms and Parliament more powers. The economy modernised and France opened towards Europe. The Empire ended in **1870** following French defeat in the war against Prussia.

## Page 43 — Third Republic and the world wars

### Third Republic: 1870–1940

The Third Republic established republican government durably. The laws of **Jules Ferry**, then Minister of Public Instruction, made public schooling **free in 1881**, then **secular and compulsory in 1882**.

Major freedom-protecting laws included:

- **29 July 1881:** press freedom.
- **21 March 1884:** trade unions authorised.
- **1 July 1901:** freedom of association.
- **9 December 1905:** separation of Churches and State.

### First World War: 1914–1918

The “Great War” initially involved European powers, including France, before spreading to other continents. It began in July 1914 and ended with the **armistice of 11 November 1918**. France, led by President of the Council **Georges Clemenceau**, and its allies—Britain, Italy and the United States—defeated Germany and its allies, including Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire.

**11 November** is a public holiday commemorating the Armistice, victory, peace and all who died for France. The source gives **10 million deaths**, including **1.45 million for France**. The war weakened Europe, changed borders, produced new countries from collapsed empires, accompanied the Bolshevik revolution in Russia and the emergence of the USSR (Union of Soviet Socialist Republics), and increased US power.

The **Treaty of Versailles, 28 June 1919**, ended the war and provided for the League of Nations, which later inspired the United Nations.

### Second World War: 1939–1945

The war was launched by **Adolf Hitler**, Nazi dictator of Germany. Germany invaded France in **1940**, occupying it partly at first and entirely from **1942**. The Nazis persecuted political opponents and exterminated millions of Jews in the **Holocaust (*Shoah*)**, as well as thousands of Roma people, homosexual people and disabled people. The source gives more than **50 million deaths**, mostly civilians.

## Page 44 — Vichy, Resistance, Liberation and Fourth Republic

### Vichy regime: 1940–1944

After France's defeat, **Marshal Pétain** established an authoritarian, undemocratic regime based in Vichy. It collaborated with Nazi Germany and shared responsibility for the deportation of Jews.

### Resistance

Part of the population rejected collaboration and resisted occupation. **Jean Moulin**, commissioned by **General de Gaulle**, leader of Free France, unified the Resistance. Moulin was arrested and tortured to death by the Nazis.

### End of the war and Liberation

Liberation began with the Allied landing in Normandy on **6 June 1944**, supported by the French Resistance. The source lists the United States, USSR, Britain and France as the Allies in this passage; this must not be read as a list of the armies landing in Normandy. [E6] **Paris was liberated on 25 August 1944**.

Germany surrendered on **8 May 1945**. The booklet calls 8 May the holiday marking the end of the Second World War; more precisely, it marks the end of the war in Europe. [E6]

### Charles de Gaulle (1890–1970)

De Gaulle led French resistance to German occupation and helped liberate France with the Allies. His **BBC appeal of 18 June 1940** marked the beginning of the Resistance in the booklet's account. He headed the Provisional Government at Liberation until **20 January 1946**. Returning to power in **1958**, he created the institutions of the Fifth Republic and became its first president.

### Provisional Government: 1944–1946; Fourth Republic: 1946–1958

The Fourth Republic began in 1946. Its Constitution gave Parliament greater weight than the executive, led by the President of the Council. This imbalance and the lack of a parliamentary majority caused governmental instability, weakening the regime until its collapse in **1958**.

## Page 45 — Fifth Republic, decolonisation and May 1968

### Fifth Republic: from 1958

The Algerian War and governmental instability caused a major political crisis. General de Gaulle returned to power in summer **1958** as President of the Council, tasked with drafting a new constitution.

Approved by referendum on **28 September 1958** and promulgated on **4 October 1958**, the Constitution established a strong executive and a president with substantial powers. To promote governmental stability and continuity, it limited the conditions under which the National Assembly could bring down the government.

De Gaulle became its first president. The Fifth Republic remains France's political system.

### French decolonisation

From the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, France conquered a large colonial empire across several continents, including West and Equatorial Africa, Algeria, Madagascar, Morocco, Tunisia and Indochina. It controlled these territories politically, administratively and economically.

After the Second World War, in which many colonial soldiers died defending France, nationalist movements demanded independence. Most colonies obtained it through negotiated processes in the **1950s and 1960s**. Two particularly deadly conflicts accompanied decolonisation: **Indochina, 1946–1954**, and **Algeria, 1954–1962**. These countries became independent after the conflicts.

### May 1968

Students demanding better study conditions and more freedom began a social movement in **May 1968**. Workers and unions broadened it into a challenge to the rigid political and social system. The strikes and demonstrations were among the century's largest. The movement produced major social gains, including an increase in the minimum wage, and transformed society.

## Page 46 — Major social changes under the Fifth Republic

### Voting age lowered to 18: 1974

One of **Valéry Giscard d'Estaing**'s first decisions after election in 1974 was to lower the age of majority and civic participation from **21 to 18**.

### Abortion decriminalised: 1975

The **Veil law of 17 January 1975**, presented by Health Minister **Simone Veil**, decriminalised abortion. It advanced women's control over their bodies, following the **Neuwirth law of 1967** authorising oral contraception. Women could choose whether and when to have children, and obtain abortions under legally regulated, medically safe conditions.

### Death penalty abolished: 1981

Justice Minister **Robert Badinter**, under President **François Mitterrand**, championed the complete abolition of the death penalty in **1981**. It marked a major human-rights milestone and remains a pillar of republican values and human dignity. The illustration shows Badinter's speech of 17 September 1981.

### Marriage for all: 2013

The law of **17 May 2013**, supported by Justice Minister **Christiane Taubira** under **François Hollande**, allowed same-sex couples to marry and adopt children. The first same-sex marriage in France took place at Montpellier town hall on **29 May 2013**.

## Page 49 — Territory, international role and economy

The booklet gives France's **2025 population as 68.6 million**, and its metropolitan and overseas area as **675,000 km²**.

Metropolitan France lies in western Europe and shares land borders with **eight countries**: Germany, Andorra, Belgium, Spain, Italy, Luxembourg, Monaco and Switzerland. It has approximately **5,500 km of coastline** on the English Channel (between France and England), North Sea, Atlantic Ocean and Mediterranean Sea.

The source lists Paris, Lyon, Lille, Marseille, Toulouse, Bordeaux, Nantes, Nice and Strasbourg as major cities with international reach as of 1 January 2025; the order is not presented here as a population ranking.

### A. France in the world

France is among the ten leading world powers. Its history, international network and ability to defend its positions give it an important role in international relations.

It is a **permanent member of the UN Security Council**, with a **veto** allowing it to block decisions. It has embassies and consulates in more than 150 countries, one of the world's largest diplomatic networks. It participates in the EU, UN, NATO, G7, G20 and International Organisation of La Francophonie, among other bodies.

**Sidebar:** The UN was created in **1945**, after the Second World War, particularly to maintain international peace and security.

### B. France's economy

Gross domestic product (**GDP; PIB in French**) reflects the wealth produced each year. For **2024**, the booklet describes France as the **seventh-largest economy**, with GDP of **US$2,830 billion**. This source figure is retained without updating or currency conversion; it is a historical source statement, not a current valuation. [E12]

France has around 100 large business groups with more than 10,000 employees, operating in many countries. **Paris La Défense** is described as Europe's largest business district, with over 500 businesses, including headquarters of many major French companies.

## Page 50 — Economic sectors, maritime activity and agriculture

Several sectors of excellence play a key role in France's international competitiveness, innovation and growth. French strengths include:

- Aeronautics and space.
- Luxury goods and fashion.
- Agrifood.
- Energy.
- Pharmaceuticals and biotechnology.
- Digital technology.
- Motor vehicles.

**Sidebar:** France plays a central role in European access to space through the European Space Agency, ArianeGroup and the **Kourou launch site in French Guiana**, from which Ariane rockets launch.

Multiple coastlines and access to major trade routes benefit the economy. The booklet states that over 90% of international goods trade travels by sea, including oil, minerals, timber, grain and containers. Major French commercial ports are **Le Havre, Marseille-Fos, Dunkerque and Nantes-Saint-Nazaire**.

France is also the EU's leading agricultural power, producing **24% of European grain**, according to the source, and holding Europe's largest cattle herd—that is, the largest number of oxen, cows, bulls and calves. It exports wheat, wine, dairy products and meat, while importing fruit, vegetables and processed foods.

- Paris Basin: wheat and sugar beet.
- Brittany and western France: dairy cattle and pigs.
- Southwest: livestock and maize.
- Southeast: fruit, vegetables and wine.
- Burgundy, Champagne, Languedoc and Bordeaux: vineyards.

## Page 51 — Mountains, rivers and volcanoes

### C. Notable geographical features

#### 1. Mountain ranges

The **Alps** stretch across Europe, separating France and Italy in particular. The booklet calls **Mont Blanc**, at **4,810 m**, the highest mountain in France and Europe. [E8] The Alps are especially known for winter ski resorts.

The **Pyrenees** lie between France and Spain. Other French ranges include the **Massif Central, Jura and Vosges**.

#### 2. Rivers — fleuves

- **Loire:** France's longest river, known for natural landscapes and châteaux.

### E. National celebrations

Annual events shape national life, form part of cultural, historical and social heritage, and bring people together. Some coincide with public holidays, including 14 July, end-of-year celebrations, Labour Day and 8 May.

#### 1. National day: 14 July

Celebrated annually since **1880**, it commemorates both:

- **14 July 1789:** storming of the Bastille, marking the Revolution—the people's revolt against the Ancien Régime—and the struggle for liberty.
- **14 July 1790:** Fête de la Fédération, affirming attachment to the Constitution.

It combines an institutional celebration, with a military parade on the Champs-Élysées in the President's presence, and popular festivities across France, including concerts, dances and fireworks.

Labour Day or Workers' Day has been a public holiday since **1947**. Its roots lie in US trade-union action on **1 May 1886** for an eight-hour working day. Unions traditionally organise demonstrations, and people offer lily-of-the-valley (*muguet*) as a good-luck symbol.

#### 3. Fête de la musique: 21 June

Launched in **1982**, this popular festival allows musicians to perform and become known, particularly in the streets. It takes place every **21 June**.

## Page 62 — Housing

### A. Housing

#### 1. Buying a home

Anyone may buy an existing property or a new property, including a construction project. A property purchase requires a deed signed before a **notaire**. Building a house requires a building-permit application to the town hall. External alterations or extensions generally require advance notification or permission; consult the town hall before work begins.

Assisted home ownership (*accession sociale à la propriété*) helps lower-income households buy their main residence.

#### 2. Renting

The source states that **40% of the population rents**, with slightly fewer than half of rented homes being public/social housing. Lettings are governed by general regulations and the specific terms of the **lease (*bail*)**, including the agreed rent.

Tenants:

- May decorate, for example by painting walls; other work requires the owner's permission, under Article 7 of Law No. **89-462 of 6 July 1989**.
- May leave after giving the required notice.
- Must pay rent and charges such as water, electricity and heating.
- May face eviction proceedings if rent is unpaid.

Owners must provide decent accommodation. The booklet describes this as safe, well maintained, well insulated and accessible to disabled people; actual legal standards have qualifications. [E11]

## Page 63 — Social housing and road rules

#### 3. Social housing

Social housing, also called **HLM (*habitations à loyer modéré*)**, helps people with limited resources. Houses and apartments are built with State financial support and must meet construction, management and tenant-selection rules. Applicants' income must stay below specified ceilings, and rents are regulated.

Apply online at https://www.demande-logement-social.gouv.fr.

### B. Transport

#### 1. Highway Code — Code de la route

Road rules apply to all users on roads, motorways, streets and boulevards. Respecting them protects everyone. The source says drivers of motor vehicles on public roads need a valid licence and vehicle registration certificate (*carte grise*), with insurance and an up-to-date technical inspection.

The source calls the registration certificate proof of ownership; this is imprecise. [E11]

Penalties depend on seriousness. The booklet gives these examples:

- Speeding by less than 20 km/h: a possible **€68 fine and one licence point**. [E11]
- Speeding by more than 50 km/h: up to **three months in prison**, a **€3,750 fine**, **six licence points**, and possible suspension.

**Question: May a person without a disability park in a disabled bay?** No. These bays are reserved for eligible people holding the *carte mobilité inclusion*.

## Page 64 — Dangerous driving, public transport and insurance

The source gives these serious driving-offence penalties:

- Blood alcohol above **0.8 g/l**: up to **three years' imprisonment**, **€9,000**, seizure of the vehicle and cancellation of the licence for five years.
- Driving without a licence: up to **one year in prison** and **€15,000**.

These are source statements rather than a complete, current penalty schedule; see [E11].

#### 2. Public transport

Buses, coaches, metros, trains and trams improve mobility. They reduce car use, energy consumption, pollution and noise, helping tackle climate change and improve air quality. Users generally must buy a ticket; non-payment can lead to a fine. Some towns offer free transport.

### C. Civil liability and insurance

**Civil liability (*responsabilité civile*)** is the obligation to compensate for material, physical or non-material harm unintentionally caused to others. A person may also be responsible for harm caused by their minor child, pet or an object under their control.

Damage can occur at home, while driving, during personal activities or at work. Insurance includes:

- **Home insurance:** fire, water damage and other housing risks.
- **Vehicle insurance:** accidents involving combustion, hybrid or electric motor vehicles.
- **School and extracurricular insurance:** protection during children's activities inside and outside school.

These policies compensate people injured or otherwise affected by accidents.

## Page 65 — Smoking and alcohol in public

### D. Living together in public spaces

#### 1. Smoking

Strict rules protect public health. Smoking is prohibited in enclosed or covered public places and indoor areas open to the public, including stations, restaurants, bars and covered terraces, and inside workplaces.

Smoking in public space is otherwise permitted subject to restrictions. The booklet states that, from **1 July 2025**, smoking is prohibited during opening periods in:

- Public parks and gardens.
- Beaches bordering bathing waters.
- Bus shelters and covered passenger waiting areas.
- Areas around schools and other places accommodating, educating or housing minors.
- Open spaces and surroundings of libraries, swimming pools, stadiums and sports facilities.

It traces these measures to the **Évin law of 10 January 1991**, subsequently extended. They limit exposure, especially for children and vulnerable people, and reduce nuisance. The source gives a **€135 fine**.

**Sidebar:** Cigarette butts are litter and must go in a bin; improper disposal can lead to a fine.

#### 2. Alcohol

Local municipal orders may prohibit alcohol consumption in particular streets, transport or parks. Sales may be restricted by place, period or time. Alcohol may not be sold to people **under 18**.

Public drunkenness can lead to a fine of up to **€150**, according to the source. Alcohol advertising is regulated by the Public Health Code. These rules protect public order, drinkers and other users of public space.

## Page 66 — Civic conduct and waste

#### 3. Other public-space rules

Litter must be put in bins. Dog waste must be collected. Non-compliance may lead to fines. Graffiti and damage to public or private property are prohibited forms of vandalism; cleaning, repairs and replacements cost communities money.

Noise nuisance, including loud music, unnecessary horn use, parties and gatherings, can be penalised to protect public tranquillity.

### E. Protecting the environment

Climate change and nature protection concern everyone. Alongside government, local-authority and association action, individuals can and must help preserve the planet for future generations.

#### 1. Reducing waste

The source estimates that each French person produces about **500 kg of waste annually**. Local authorities organise collection and sorting, with rules that vary by area. It states that sorting green and food waste for composting has been compulsory for individuals and professionals since **2024**. Residents should learn and follow their municipality's arrangements.

Waste collection centres (*déchèteries*) accept appliances, bulky items and garden waste. Dumping waste in streets or nature outside authorised collection arrangements is illegal and may be fined.

**Sidebar:** The booklet says single-use, non-recyclable plastic bags have been prohibited since January 2017.

## Page 67 — Energy and greener travel

#### 2. Limiting energy use

Energy consumption affects the environment through greenhouse-gas emissions. The source suggests everyday actions to reduce emissions and bills:

- Set heating to a maximum of **19°C**.
- Set the water heater to **55°C** and take shorter showers.
- Turn off unused devices, including those on standby.
- Use electrical appliances outside peak periods.
- Install a programmable thermostat.

#### 3. Greener travel

The source attributes **34% of French CO₂ emissions** to transport, with nearly half of that from private vehicles. Public transport is an alternative to individual car use, especially in cities. Walk or cycle for short trips.

If driving, accelerate and drive smoothly to save fuel, and share rides. Choosing a cleaner vehicle and maintaining it, including tyre pressure and oil changes, can reduce fuel use and pollution.

## Page 68 — Health

### F. Health

National solidarity at Liberation led to the creation of **Social Security (*Sécurité sociale*) in 1945**. Employer, employee and self-employed contributions, together with taxes, finance it. The system expresses solidarity and fraternity.

Anyone living in France can consult a doctor, attend hospital or visit a pharmacy. Residents must be registered with **Assurance Maladie**, the health-insurance branch of Social Security, according to their situation and legal eligibility.

Insured people receive a **Carte Vitale** to demonstrate entitlement. The **CPAM (*Caisse primaire d'assurance maladie*)** reimburses healthcare costs. With **tiers payant**, patients do not have to advance the part reimbursed by Assurance Maladie.

Some costs are not fully covered. A **mutuelle**, or supplementary health-insurance policy, can help pay the remainder. Employers must offer collective supplementary cover to employees, subject to exceptions.

Health professionals must keep patients' medical information confidential.

## Page 70 — Working in France: employment rules

### A. Employment regulation

Employment law governs relations between workers and employers. Three kinds of document define the rules:

- **Code du travail:** rights and obligations concerning contracts, working conditions, pay and the **35-hour statutory working week**, among other matters.
- **Conventions collectives:** sector-specific collective agreements negotiated by employers' and employees' organisations, adapting rules such as leave or minimum pay to the activity concerned; road transport is the example given.
- **Règlement intérieur:** internal rules drawn up by employers with at least **50 employees**, covering health, safety and workplace conduct.

The source states that private-sector employees aged at least 18—or 16 with parental authorisation, or emancipated minors—must receive at least the **SMIC (*salaire minimum de croissance*)**, the guaranteed minimum hourly wage. The statement is general; special rules can apply to young workers or particular contracts.

Workplace discrimination based on gender, religion or appearance is prohibited. Employers may require qualifications and ask to see diplomas where relevant to a job.

The **Conseil de prud'hommes** settles individual disputes between employers and employees.

**Sidebar:** Every worker may join a trade union to represent their interests.

## Page 71 — Contracts, finding work and business creation

### B. Employment contracts

The source states that an employee and employer must sign an employment contract and calls this document compulsory. [E9] After hiring following an interview, this document defines working conditions: pay, required skills (what the employee needs to know how to do), starting date, working time (hours per week, quarter, year, etc.) and contract type.

Common contracts are:

- **CDD (*contrat à durée déterminée*):** fixed-term work.
- **CDI (*contrat à durée indéterminée*):** employment without a predetermined end date.
- **Intérim:** temporary agency work.

### C. Entering or returning to employment

Register with **France Travail** for help finding work and, if eligible, benefits after job loss. This public body provides tailored advice and may offer training. **Missions Locales** support young people, described in the booklet as those under 25.

People who meet conditions, such as having worked long enough and not voluntarily leaving their employment, may receive **Allocation d'aide au retour à l'emploi (ARE)** each month.

### D. Starting a business

French nationality is not necessary to start a business. Administrative formalities may include registering articles of association, registering the business, publishing a legal notice and declaring its registered office. Requirements depend on the activity and legal form.

## Page 72 — Business sectors and public employment

Four broad business sectors are distinguished:

- **Crafts (*artisanat*):** independent production, processing or service activities.
- **Commerce:** buying for resale, intermediary operations, goods transport and other commercial activities.
- **Liberal professions (*professions libérales*):** mainly non-commercial professional services, such as doctors or lawyers.
- **Agriculture.**

Organisations throughout France advise prospective entrepreneurs, help them plan projects and seek funding, including **Chambres de métiers et de l'artisanat (CMA)** and **Chambres de commerce et d'industrie (CCI)**.

### E. Public employment

Public employment is accessible without discrimination, with selection based on skills and qualities. The source describes competitive-examination requirements concerning age and qualifications, French nationality or nationality of an EU or European Economic Area (EEA) country, enjoyment of civic rights, and no conviction recorded in bulletin No. 2 of the criminal record. Some of these requirements have legal qualifications; see [E11].

Other European nationals cannot hold positions involving sovereignty, such as certain defence or foreign-affairs roles. In local-government service, unlike State civil service, passing an examination does not automatically secure a job: successful candidates join a list and must apply for positions themselves.

## Page 73 — Family life: marriage and parents' rights

### A. Marriage

Only civil marriage at a town hall is legally recognised. A mayor or deputy mayor performs it in a municipality where one partner or one partner's parent lives. The couple receives a French marriage certificate and **livret de famille**.

Marriage is possible from **18**. The booklet also says from 16 with exceptional permission from the public prosecutor and parental agreement, citing Civil Code Articles 145 and 148; this wording needs legal qualification. [E11]

**Polygamy**, being married to more than one person at once, is prohibited and criminally punishable. The **17 May 2013** law opened marriage to same-sex couples; France became the fourteenth country to do so.

Spouses may divorce through a judge or by mutual consent with a notaire. Both parents continue exercising parental authority unless a court decides otherwise.

**Sidebar — état civil:** Civil status records official life events. The municipal service, or the Ministry of Foreign Affairs for relevant foreign-born persons, draws up the documents. Births must normally be declared within **five days**, and marriages and deaths must also be registered.

### B. Parents' rights

Pregnancy and maternity leave are protected: an employer cannot dismiss a woman because she is pregnant or on maternity leave.

The source says paternity leave has lasted **28 days since 1 July 2021**. [E13] After a birth or adoption of a child under 16, an employee may obtain parental education leave to stop or reduce work to raise the child, subject to applicable conditions.

The booklet also describes **additional birth leave** of one or two months, chosen by each parent, for children born or adopted from **1 January 2026**, usable from **July 2026**. This is a source statement; individual eligibility and implementation details should be checked before use.

## Page 74 — Children's rights and parental duties

### Children's rights

The source says 196 states signed the **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** in **1989**. [E14] It sets out children's fundamental rights and obligations for states, and underpins UNICEF's work. UNICEF is an organisation within the UN system.

Children have rights to:

- Equality.
- A name and identity.
- Healthcare, protection from illness and adequate, balanced nutrition.
- Schooling.
- Privacy.
- Protection from violence and exploitation.
- Information, expression and a hearing on matters affecting them.
- An equal life when disabled, including adapted timetables where appropriate.

### Parental duties

**Article 371-1 of the Civil Code** defines parental authority as rights and duties protecting the child's interests and welfare until adulthood or emancipation. Parents must protect safety, health, privacy and moral development; provide education; and support development with respect for the child.

In practice, parents supervise relationships, movements, communications and social-media use, protecting children from extortion, rape, prostitution, addiction, bullying and violence. They provide food, housing, recreation and healthcare and protect privacy and image rights.

Parental authority must be exercised **without physical or psychological violence**. Female genital mutilation, corporal punishment and other physical abuse are prohibited because they violate bodily integrity and dignity. Children participate in decisions affecting them according to age and maturity.

**Sidebar:** The booklet states that children cannot be entirely disinherited and that inheritance must be fairly shared between siblings. This is a simplified description of inheritance protection, not a complete statement of succession law.

Parents are part of the educational community and maintain contact each school year with teachers, administration, nursery-school assistants (ATSEM), extracurricular staff and others. They may stand as parent representatives or join a parents' association.

## Page 78 — Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, 1789

The following is an unofficial translation of the 17 articles reproduced in the booklet.

**Article 1.** Human beings are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be based only on the common good.

**Article 2.** The purpose of every political association is to preserve the natural and imprescriptible rights of human beings. These rights are liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression.

**Article 3.** The principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation. No body and no individual may exercise authority that does not expressly derive from it.

**Article 4.** Liberty consists in being able to do anything that does not harm others. The exercise of each person's natural rights has no limits other than those that ensure other members of society enjoy the same rights. These limits may be determined only by law.

**Article 5.** The law may prohibit only actions harmful to society. Anything not prohibited by law may not be prevented, and nobody may be compelled to do what the law does not require.

**Article 6.** The law expresses the general will. All citizens have the right to participate personally, or through representatives, in making it. It must be the same for everyone, whether protecting or punishing. All citizens, equal in its eyes, are equally eligible for public honours, positions and employment according to their ability, with no distinction other than their virtues and talents.

**Article 7.** Nobody may be accused, arrested or detained except in cases determined by law and according to its prescribed procedures. Those who request, issue, execute or cause arbitrary orders to be executed must be punished. Any citizen summoned or arrested under the law must obey immediately; resistance makes them culpable.

**Article 8.** The law must establish only penalties that are strictly and evidently necessary. Nobody may be punished except under a law established and promulgated before the offence and lawfully applied.

**Article 9.** Everyone is presumed innocent until declared guilty. If arrest is considered indispensable, any severity unnecessary to secure the person must be severely punished by law.

**Article 10.** Nobody may be troubled for their opinions, including religious opinions, provided their expression does not disturb public order established by law.

**Article 11.** Free communication of thoughts and opinions is one of humanity's most precious rights. Every citizen may speak, write and print freely, while remaining answerable for abuses of this freedom in cases defined by law.

**Article 12.** Guaranteeing human and citizens' rights requires a public force. This force is established for everyone's benefit, not the private advantage of those entrusted with it.

**Article 13.** A common contribution is essential to maintain the public force and meet administrative expenses. It must be distributed equally among citizens according to their means.

**Article 14.** All citizens have the right, personally or through representatives, to determine the need for public taxation, freely consent to it, oversee its use and determine its amount, basis, collection and duration.

**Article 15.** Society has the right to require every public official to account for their administration.

**Article 16.** A society in which rights are not guaranteed and the separation of powers is not established has no constitution.

**Article 17.** Property is an inviolable and sacred right. Nobody may be deprived of it unless legally established public necessity clearly requires it, and then only with fair compensation paid beforehand.

## Page 79 — Charter of the Rights and Duties of the French Citizen

French Republic — Charter of the Rights and Duties of the French Citizen.

Under Article 21-24 of the Civil Code, this charter recalls the Republic's essential principles and values and sets out citizens' constitutional and legal rights and duties.

### Principles, values and symbols

The French people identify with the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 26 August 1789** and the democratic principles inherited from their history. They respect republican symbols:

- National emblem: the blue, white and red tricolour.
- National anthem: *La Marseillaise*.
- Motto: *Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité*.
- National day: 14 July.
- Marianne: symbolic representation of the Republic.
- Language of the Republic: French.

France is an indivisible, secular, democratic and social Republic, whose principles are established by the **Constitution of 4 October 1958**.

**Indivisible:** National sovereignty belongs to the people, exercised through elected representatives and referendum. No section of the people and no individual may claim its exercise.

**Secular:** The Republic ensures freedom of conscience and respects all beliefs. Everyone may believe, not believe or change religion. The Republic guarantees freedom of worship but recognises, pays or subsidises no religion. State and religions are separate.

**Democratic:** The Republic's principle is government of the people, by the people and for the people. Suffrage, direct or indirect, is always universal, equal and secret. Since law expresses the general will, every citizen must respect it. Nobody may be forced to do what law does not require. Justice is administered independently in the French people's name. The public force ensures compliance with laws and court decisions.

**Social:** The nation ensures the conditions necessary for individuals and families to develop.

The Republic guarantees everyone's personal and property security. It participates in the EU, whose states have freely chosen to exercise some responsibilities together.

## Page 80 — Charter continued: rights and duties

Every human being, without distinction of race, religion or belief, possesses inalienable rights. Within the Republic these are guaranteed to everyone, and everyone must respect them.

French citizenship also entails specific rights and duties, including participating in the election of the people's representatives, national defence and criminal juries.

### Liberty

- Human beings are born and remain free and equal in rights.
- Liberty means being able to do anything that does not harm others.
- Respect for the person forbids violations of dignity. The human body is inviolable.
- Nobody may be troubled for their opinions, provided their expression does not disturb public order.
- Every citizen may speak, write and print freely, while answering for abuse of this freedom in cases provided by law.
- Everyone has a right to privacy.
- Nobody may be accused, arrested or detained except in cases and according to procedures established by law. Everyone is presumed innocent until found guilty.
- Everyone may create or join associations, freely join political parties or groups, and defend rights and interests through trade-union action.
- French citizens aged 18 who retain civic rights may vote. Eligible voters may stand for election under legal conditions. Voting is both a right and a civic duty.
- Everyone is entitled to respect for property they own.

### Equality

- All citizens are equal before the law regardless of sex, origin, race or religion. The law is the same for all, whether protecting or punishing.
- Women and men have the same rights in every field.
- The Republic promotes equal access for women and men to elected offices and professional and social responsibilities.
- Each spouse may freely work, receive income and use it as they choose after contributing to shared expenses.
- Parents exercise parental authority jointly, provide children's education and prepare their future.
- Instruction is compulsory for children of both sexes until 16. Organising free, secular public education at all levels is a State duty.
- French citizens, being equal, may access public employment according to their abilities.

### Fraternity

- Every French citizen contributes to national defence and cohesion.
- The charter states that a person who acquired French nationality may be deprived of it for evading defence obligations or acts contrary to France's fundamental interests. This is a general charter statement; the legal conditions are restrictive and are not fully set out here.
- Everyone must contribute to national expenditure according to financial means through taxes and social contributions.
- The nation guarantees health protection, material security and entitlement to leave. Anyone unable to work because of age, physical or mental condition, or economic circumstances is entitled to adequate means of subsistence from the community.
